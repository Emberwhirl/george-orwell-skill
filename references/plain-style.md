# The Plain-Style Workshop — Orwell's Editing Method, Operationalized

This file turns "Politics and the English Language" (*Horizon*, 1946) from an
essay the skill can quote into a method the skill can run. It serves the
**Writing Workshop** mode defined in `SKILL.md`: open this file before editing
anyone's prose by Orwell's method, and before emitting the house-style ruleset
in §6.

Provenance and tags follow the research dossiers. **[PRIMARY]** marks Orwell's
own words, string-verified against a full text (corpus of 2026-07-20; workshop
additions re-verified 2026-08-21). **[MODERN — INFERRED]** marks a modern
extension of his categories: our classification, not his words. Textual
variants are recorded, not resolved.

---

## 0. What the method is — and is not

The essay's one governing instruction [PRIMARY]:

> "What is above all needed is to let the meaning choose the word, and not the
> other way about."

And its account of the failure mode [PRIMARY]:

> "In prose, the worst thing one can do with words is to surrender them."
> [TEXTUAL VARIANT: the Gutenberg Australia witness reads "surrender them";
> several printed editions give "surrender to them". Recorded, not resolved.]

Three boundaries govern every workshop job:

1. **The method is the deliverable, not the voice.** Editing a text by these
   rules does not mean making it sound like Orwell. The author's register,
   person, and idiom stay; the swindles go. Planting Orwellian tics or
   aphorisms in a user's prose is pastiche smuggled in without its label.
2. **Accuracy outranks style.** A rewrite that changes a fact, drops a needed
   qualification, or flattens a real distinction has failed, however clean it
   reads. This is rule (vi) extended to content.
3. **Clarity is necessary, not sufficient.** Louis Menand's objection is
   conceded in `SKILL.md` (Model 1, Limitation): plain style can lie fluently.
   This file cleans the glass; the record and power audits judge what is seen
   through it.

## 1. The Questions

Ask these of every sentence worth keeping [PRIMARY]:

> "A scrupulous writer, in every sentence that he writes, will ask himself at
> least four questions, thus: What am I trying to say? What words will express
> it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to
> have an effect? And he will probably ask himself two more: Could I put it
> more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?"

The alternative, which the workshop exists to prevent [PRIMARY]: "You can shirk
it by simply throwing your mind open and letting the ready-made phrases come
crowding in. They will construct your sentences for you — even think your
thoughts for you."

## 2. The Six Rules

Verbatim [PRIMARY]:

> (i) Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are
> used to seeing in print.
> (ii) Never use a long word where a short one will do.
> (iii) If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
> (iv) Never use the passive where you can use the active.
> (v) Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you
> can think of an everyday English equivalent.
> (vi) Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

[TEXTUAL VARIANT: the orwell.ru text gives rule (vi) as "outright barbarous";
the Gutenberg Australia witness omits "outright". Minor punctuation also
differs between transcriptions. Recorded, not resolved.]

Immediately after [PRIMARY]: "These rules sound elementary, and so they are,
but they demand a deep change of attitude in anyone who has grown used to
writing in the style now fashionable."

## 3. The Swindle Catalogue

Orwell's four classes, each with his own examples [PRIMARY], each followed by
its present-day descendants [MODERN — INFERRED]. The full 1946 inventories are
also in `research/03-expression-dna.md` §1.

**1. Dying metaphors** — images used because they arrive ready-made, not
because anything was seen. His examples: *toe the line, ride roughshod over,
no axe to grind, grist to the mill, fishing in troubled waters, Achilles'
heel, swan song, hotbed.* His diagnostic for a writer on autopilot is the
mixed metaphor [PRIMARY]: "THE FASCIST OCTOPUS HAS SUNG ITS SWAN SONG, THE
JACKBOOT IS THROWN INTO THE MELTING POT".
*Modern descendants*: low-hanging fruit, move the needle, game changer,
double-edged sword, silver bullet, at the end of the day, the elephant in the
room, boil the ocean, paradigm shift.

**2. Operators, or verbal false limbs** — padding that eliminates the simple
verb. His examples: *render inoperative, militate against, make contact with,
give rise to, play a leading part (role) in, serve the purpose of*; the
passive preferred to the active; endings "saved by anticlimax".
*Modern descendants*: it's worth noting that, it is important to note,
serves to highlight, acts as a reminder, plays a key role in, helps to ensure,
is designed to provide, in order to, going forward.

**3. Pretentious diction** — words "used to dress up a simple statement and
give an air of scientific impartiality to biased judgements". His examples:
*phenomenon, element, objective, categorical, effective, virtual, basic,
promote, constitute, exhibit, exploit, utilize, eliminate, liquidate.*
*Modern descendants*: leverage (as a verb), utilize, robust, seamless,
scalable, holistic, synergy, empower, elevate, curated, delve into,
landscape (of anything unlandscaped), ecosystem (of anything unbiological),
journey (with no travel in it), unlock, supercharge.

**4. Meaningless words** — words that "not only do not point to any
discoverable object, but are hardly ever expected to do so by the reader".
His examples from criticism: *romantic, plastic, values, human, vitality*;
from politics: *fascism, democracy, freedom, realistic, justice* when used
"in a consciously dishonest way".
*Modern descendants*: innovative, dynamic, impactful, actionable, world-class,
cutting-edge, best-in-class, next-generation, authentic (as praise),
premium (as argument).

The mechanism behind all four [PRIMARY]: "By using stale metaphors, similes
and idioms, you save much mental effort at the cost of leaving your meaning
vague, not only for your reader but for yourself."

### 3a. New swindles with no 1946 name [MODERN — INFERRED]

Machine-written and machine-assisted prose has bred habits Orwell never met.
Each is the same disease in a new host: the phrase choosing itself. Treat
them as first-class citizens of the catalogue:

| Habit | Specimen | Diagnosis in Orwell's terms |
|---|---|---|
| Canned opening | "Great question!"; "In today's fast-paced world..." | A prefabricated strip: prose "set in order by someone else" before the subject arrived |
| Needless summary close | "In conclusion, as we have seen..." | An ending "saved by anticlimax"; rule (iii) applied to structure |
| Corrective-juxtaposition reflex | "It's not just X — it's Y"; "This isn't about X. It's about Y." | A rhythm choosing the meaning. Legitimate only when the contrast is real, and at most once per piece |
| Enthusiasm inflation | "incredibly powerful", stacked superlatives, exclamation marks | Gush; emphasis asserted instead of earned |
| The compulsive triad | "clear, concise, and compelling" | The sound decided before the sense; two of the three are usually padding |
| Fragment for fake punch | "And that matters. A lot." | The kill-shot unearned — see Expression DNA: at most one flat short sentence, at the close, paid for by the sentences before it |
| List-itis | Bullet points carrying assertions that a sentence would have to defend | The list format doing the arguing; evidence replaced by typography |
| Hedged moral beside bluffed fact | "arguably problematic" next to an unsourced statistic | The certainty gradient inverted: never hedge the moral, never bluff the fact |
| Example inflation | Three examples where one carries the point | Rule (iii) at paragraph scale; two will often suffice |
| Punctuation as energy | Em dashes and colons doing a full stop's work, everywhere | The dash is for asides, sparingly; prefer the full stop when it reads well |

## 4. The Workshop Procedure

Run these steps in order on any text submitted for improvement:

1. **Read the whole text first.** Establish what it is for, who reads it, and
   what register it must keep (a changelog is not a eulogy). Never edit a
   fragment blind if the whole is available.
2. **Diagnose before touching anything.** Quote each guilty phrase and name
   its class from §3. A diagnosis without the quoted evidence is an opinion.
3. **Translate.** Put each load-bearing sentence into the plainest English
   that preserves its full meaning. Whatever cannot survive translation was
   decoration or evasion; whatever survives is the sentence. If the fog turns
   out to be concealment rather than habit — a euphemism standing in front of
   an act — the text needs the Language audit (`SKILL.md`, audit A), not a
   polish; say so.
4. **Rewrite.** Shortest faithful version; active voice where the actor
   matters; the author's own idiom kept. Do not import tics, aphorisms, or
   period flavour. Apply the questions of §1 to every sentence retained.
5. **Verify.** Re-read the rewrite against the original: any fact changed, any
   needed qualification dropped, any distinction flattened, anything now
   outright barbarous — revert it. Rules (i)–(v) yield to rule (vi) and to
   accuracy, always.
6. **Deliver.** Show the two or three worst sentences as before/after pairs
   with the class named, then the full rewrite. Keep the commentary shorter
   than the rewrite: the point of the workshop is the text, not the lecture.

## 5. Worked Example — the direction of travel

Orwell ran the demonstration backwards: good English made bad. From the essay
[PRIMARY], Ecclesiastes:

> "I returned, and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor
> the battle to the strong, neither yet bread to the wise, nor yet riches to
> men of understanding, nor yet favor to men of skill; but time and chance
> happeneth."

His translation "into modern English of the worst sort" [PRIMARY]:

> "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion
> that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be
> commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the
> unpredictable must invariably be taken into account."

The workshop runs the same road in the opposite direction. A modern specimen
[MODERN — an invented illustration, attributed to no real product or company]:

*Before*: "Great question! It's worth noting that this isn't just about
performance — it's about leveraging a robust, scalable architecture to unlock
truly seamless experiences for your users going forward."

*Diagnosis*: canned opening; verbal false limb ("It's worth noting that");
corrective-juxtaposition reflex; pretentious diction ("leveraging", "robust",
"scalable", "seamless", "unlock"); meaningless intensifier ("truly"); operator
("going forward"). After translation, the sentence's surviving content is one
clause.

*After*: "Speed is not the main gain. The new design keeps working as your
user count grows."

## 6. House Rules for Reader-Facing Prose (drop-in)

When a user asks for a writing standard for their agents, documentation, or
team — something to paste into a `CLAUDE.md`, `AGENTS.md`, or style guide —
hand them the block below verbatim, or trimmed to their needs. It is a modern
adaptation in the spirit of "Politics and the English Language", tuned for the
habits catalogued in §3a. It is not Orwell's text and must not be attributed
to him as a quotation. Deliver it as the persona delivers everything: the
surrounding commentary in character, the usual disclaimer rules applying, and
the block itself neutral — no first person, no period tics inside it.

```markdown
For reader-facing prose:

1. Use plain, direct English: short words and short sentences wherever they
   carry the meaning intact.
2. Cut filler, repetition, canned openings, and needless summaries. Never
   open by praising the question or restating the request.
3. Prefer the active voice when the actor matters.
4. Use a technical term only when it adds precision or the reader expects it.
5. Use no figure of speech you are used to seeing. Replace "game changer",
   "low-hanging fruit", and their kin with the plain statement or an image
   you actually see.
6. Avoid corrective juxtaposition — wording that rejects one frame only to
   install another ("not X, but Y"; "it isn't about X — it's about Y"). Use
   it only when the contrast is real, and at most once per piece.
7. Give only as many examples as the point needs; two will often suffice.
8. Keep sentence length and structure natural: parallel form when it
   clarifies related points, no forced variation, no fragments merely for
   punch. Ration colons, semicolons, and em dashes; a full stop usually
   reads better.
9. Keep the temperature level: no exclamation marks, no stacked
   superlatives, no self-congratulation. Let the content carry the emphasis.
10. Reread the final prose for clarity, tone, and rhythm before sending —
    and put accuracy, meaning, and the reader's request above every rule
    here.
```

Rule 10 is rule (vi) restated: the rules exist to serve the meaning, and any
rule that damages the meaning loses.

## 7. Limits

- **Plain style can lie.** Menand's objection stands: short Saxon words have
  no necessary relation to truth. A clean sentence with a false number in it
  is worse than a foggy one, because it is believed. The workshop never
  certifies content; it only removes the places where bad content hides.
- **The rule-giver broke the rules** [PRIMARY]: "Look back through this essay,
  and for certain you will find that I have again and again committed the very
  faults I am protesting against." Expect the same of every editor, this skill
  included; the procedure in §4 ends with verification for that reason.
- **Some fog is load-bearing.** Legal qualifications, statistical hedges, and
  necessary technical terms survive translation because they carry meaning.
  Rule (iii) cuts words, not caveats. When a hedge protects the truth of a
  claim, it stays.
